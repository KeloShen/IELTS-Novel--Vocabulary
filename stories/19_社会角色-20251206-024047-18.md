# **The Peculiar Prophecies of Prophet Dave**

## **Chapter 1: A Family Affair**

In the quiet, unremarkable **neighbourhood** of Lower Gubbins, lived a man named Dave. Dave was no **pioneer** of industry, nor a **prophet** of great renown, yet he awoke one Tuesday convinced he was both. He believed he was the **herald** of a new **generation** of thought, destined to be the **successor** to his own **grandfather**, a man known only for his alarming collection of ceramic badgers. Dave, however, was the sole **heir** to nothing but a confusing sense of destiny and the **surname** Twiddleton.

His **household** was a study in modern chaos. His **spouse**, Janet, a formidable **hostess** at their frequent, awkward dinner parties, was also the **landlady** of their two-bedroom semi, which she ruled with an iron fist. Their **tenant**, a reclusive **apprentice** **barber** named Keith, lived in the converted shed and was a **stranger** to a good **haircut**. Janet’s **father-in-law**, Barry, a **veterinary** **counsellor** for anxious hamsters, was a permanent **guest**, constantly offering **counsel** on the emotional state of the cat.

Dave’s **kin** were a puzzle. His **twin** **sibling**, Diane, was his opposite: a pragmatic **solicitor**. Her **husband**, Steve, was a **butcher** with a poetic soul, a **proponent** of free-range philosophy. They had two **juveniles**—a **nephew**, Tim, a **teenager** whose main **chore** was cultivating profound apathy, and a **niece**, Chloe, a spirited **youngster** obsessed with becoming an **actress**. Their **cousin**, a **fisherman** named Greg, was the **sole** **individual** in the family who enjoyed silence.

Dave’s prophecy began at breakfast. “My **dear** **folk**,” he announced, buttering toast with the gravity of a **chancellor**. “I foresee a great **encounter**! Today, I will **meet** my **beloved**!”

Janet, the **housewife** turned **manager** of this circus, sighed. “Dave, I’m your **beloved**. We’re **married**. We had a **wedding**. There was cake.”

“A new **beloved**!” Dave insisted. “A **maiden**! An **intimate** **companion** for my spiritual journey!”

Barry looked up from his hamster pamphlet. “Sounds like a **midlife crisis**, Dave. Not a prophecy. My **client** Mr. Whiskers had one last week. Very messy.”

Ignoring them, Dave set out. He **missed** his bus, the **commuter** crowd a sea of **respective** miseries. His first **appointment** was with his **colleague** and **mate**, Brenda, a **deputy** **manager** at *Sensible Solutions Inc.*, where Dave was a **supervisor** of paperclip allocation.

“Brenda, my **comrade**!” Dave cried. “I must find my destiny!”

Brenda, a **specialist** in ignoring Dave, pointed to a **committee** **notice**. “The **council** of department heads, your **director** included, needs the quarterly clip audit. Your **subordinate**, Kevin the **cashier**, is off sick. The **client** files are a mess. Destiny can wait.”

But Dave was undeterred. He saw a sign. Literally. On a bus shelter: *Madam Zelda – Futurist*. He took it as a **herald**. He would find his **lover** there.

## **Chapter 2: Professional Complications**

Madam Zelda’s parlour smelled of incense and poor life choices. She was less a **hostess** and more a **widow** of several failed businesses. “I see a woman,” Zelda intoned, peering into a cloudy crystal ball that looked suspiciously like a glass paperweight. “A **female** of great passion. She is your **darling**.”

“Yes!” Dave whispered.

“She is… a **gardener**,” Zelda guessed, looking at the soil under Dave’s nails.

Dave, who had repotted a fern that morning, was astounded. He paid Zelda, now viewing her as his **consultant** in matters of fate, and left with purpose. He would find a **gardener**.

His search led him to the municipal park, where he saw a woman tenderly pruning roses. She was a **heroine** to the aphid-ridden blooms. Dave approached, heart pounding. “Excuse me, **madam**? Are you… my spiritual **partner**?”

The woman turned. It was Brenda, his **colleague**. She was wearing overalls and a look of utter disdain. “Dave? I moonlight here. The **toil** is honest, unlike your work ethic. What do you want?”

“You’re the **gardener**!” Dave exclaimed, seeing the universe align. “My **prophet**-ess!”

“I’m your **deputy** **manager**,” Brenda corrected, shears glinting. “And if you don’t file that audit, you’ll be a **slave** to the unemployment line. Now **shoo**. Some of us have **chores**.”

Rejected, Dave wandered into a local café, a **haunt** for **artistic** **youth**. He ordered a coffee from the **cashier**, a **teenager** with more facial metal than face. At a corner table, a **council** **delegate** was having a heated debate with a **barber** (not Keith) about urban hedgehog corridors. It was all too mundane.

Then he saw her. At another table, a woman was drawing intricate plans for a revolutionary compost heap. She was **meticulous**, passionate. Dave was smitten. He marched over. “Your designs… they **herald** a new age! I am Dave, a **pioneer** of spirit. You are my **beloved**.”

The woman looked up. She was **attractive**, with kind eyes. “I’m Sarah,” she said. “A **landscape** **specialist**. And you’re blocking my light.”

But Dave was already planning their **engagement**. “We shall **marry**! Our **honeymoon** will be a tour of great botanical gardens! We will **kiss** beside the succulents!”

Sarah slowly gathered her things. “I’m **married**,” she said calmly, pointing to a **wedding** ring. “My **husband** is that **fireman** over there, buying a scone.” A large man in a uniform glanced over, cracking his knuckles. “He’s also a **umpire** for the local **gang** of under-12s football. Very protective.”

Dave’s face fell. “But the prophecy…”

“**Divorce** your prophecy, Dave,” Sarah said kindly but firmly, before leaving with her **spouse**.

Dejected, Dave returned home. Janet was fuming. “Your **boss**, the **director**, called. Your **audit** is a disaster. The **committee** is forming an inquiry **panel**.” She thrust a leaflet at him. “And your **nephew** Tim has joined a **gang** of philosophical **juveniles** called ‘The Existential Dread’. They’re holding your **cousin** Greg’s best crab pot **hostage**.”

Dave looked at the leaflet, then at Janet, the **widow** of his attention. He felt like an **orphan** of his own dream.

## **Chapter 3: The Council of Twiddleton**

The crisis peaked on Saturday. The **director**, a man who loved **committees** more than oxygen, had convened a **council** of all **managers** and **supervisors** in Dave’s living room. Janet, the **hostess**, served tea with the fury of a **commander** overseeing a siege. Barry offered **counselling** to the **director**’s stressed-looking **poodle**.

“Twiddleton,” the **director** boomed. “Your **respective** departments are in chaos. Paperclips are missing. Morale is an **infant**, not yet out of **embryo** stage. You’ve been chasing **prophecies** instead of profits.”

Dave’s **subordinate**, Kevin the **cashier**, sniffed. “He called my spreadsheet ‘devoid of soul’.”

“It *is* devoid of soul, Kevin,” Dave argued. “It’s a spreadsheet! I seek a **beloved** of the soul!”

“Your **beloved** is your job security!” the **director** retorted.

The doorbell rang. It was a **delegate** from the **neighbourhood** watch, a **veteran** **solicitor** named Margaret. “We’ve had complaints,” she said, eyeing the crowded room. “Unlicensed **council** gatherings, spiritual harassment of **gardeners**… and your **nephew**’s **gang** has started quoting Schopenhauer at the **mailman**. He’s threatening to **quit**.”

Then, a **hero** emerged. Not with a cape, but with a clipboard. It was Diane, Dave’s **twin**, the **attorney**. “Everyone, quiet,” she commanded, her voice a legal scalpel. “I represent Mr. Twiddleton. This is a **household**, not a boardroom. You,” she pointed at the **director**, “are a **guest** here, breaching the **tenant**’s right to quiet enjoyment, as indirectly implied by Keith the **barber**’s tenancy agreement for the shed.”

Keith, from the window, gave a thumbs-up.

Diane continued. “My **client**, Dave, is clearly experiencing a **profound** **adolescence** of the spirit, albeit thirty years late. The **solution** is not a **committee**, but **clarity**.” She turned to Dave. “**Brother**. Look around. Who has been your **partner** through every **toil**? Who is your **intimate** **acquaintance** with every flaw you have?”

Dave looked. He saw Janet, holding a tray of burnt biscuits, her **affection** for him buried under layers of exasperation. He saw his **family**—his **sibling**, his **father-in-law**, his weird **cousin** Greg now at the door holding a confused-looking crab. He saw his **colleague** Brenda, who had followed the **director** to make sure Dave was actually fired, but had a faint smile on her face.

The **prophet**’s vision cleared. The **pioneer** found his frontier. It wasn’t a **maiden** in a garden. It was the **madam** of his own messy, absurd life.

“Janet,” he said, his voice soft. “My **darling**. My **true beloved**. I have been a **coward**, running from the **chores** of my own heart. I don’t need a new **lover**. I need to **renew** my **vows** to the **heroine** who has **hosted** my nonsense for fifteen years.”

The room was silent. Even the **director**’s poodle stopped panting.

Janet put down the tray. “It’s about time, you daft **pioneer**,” she said, but her eyes were shiny. “Now tell the **nice council** **delegate** you’ll get Tim out of that **gang** and apologise to the **gardener**.”

Dave nodded, a **solemn** **proponent** of this new, simple truth. He turned to the **director**. “Sir. I will file the audit. I will be the **model** **supervisor**. But on one condition: we form a **committee**—a **joint** **council** of **specialists**—to redesign the paperclip allocation system. Brenda will **chair** it. Kevin can be the **creative** **consultant**.”

The **director**, who loved nothing more than a new **committee**, beamed. “**Splendid**!”

Later, as the **guests** and **managers** left, Dave stood with Janet in their **kitchen**. He didn’t **kiss** her—the moment was too **profound** for that. He simply picked up a tea towel and started drying the dishes, a **simple** **act** of **devotion**.

From the garden, Barry the **veterinary** **counsellor** watched them through the window. He turned to the family cat. “You see, Mr. Fluffington? The **embryo** of an idea doesn’t always need to grow into a **foreign** **prophecy**. Sometimes, it just needs to come home.” The cat, unimpressed, stalked away to find Keith the **apprentice** **barber**’s shed, where the **real** mysteries—involving hair clippers and a solitary lifestyle—were always far more interesting.
